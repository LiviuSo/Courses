**What is disease?**

Ayurveda’s understanding of disease is similar to that of Hippocrates. For Hippocrates, good health is the body’s natural state as it is always striving to preserve its well-being. Unfortunately, there are times when aggressions to the body are so frequent that it is unable to repair itself. In fact, according to Hippocrates, disease results from small errors, anomalies, imbalances, and assaults that are repeated almost daily.

In Ayurveda, it is thought that doshic imbalances settle in the body after having been repeated a
number of times. This is because the body naturally fights against threats by activating other doshas in order to compensate for one in a state of excess. In the case of an excess of Pitta, for example, the body activates its Vata and Kapha components. But, if these doshas are too weak, more severe signs of imbalances will begin to be felt.

And the more we wait, the greater the imbalance becomes and the more the situation becomes difficult to restore. That is why in Ayurveda, we focus not on treating the disease, but on maintaining health. Ayurveda also teaches us that there are six stages of disease. Without them, we would not be able to detect early signs of a problem. Understanding these six stages helps us take charge of our health and stop the imbalance before it develops into disease!

When imbalances are still minor, we often observe that they are caused by external factors and that we simply have to stop or modify our behavior in order to re-balance ourselves. For this reason, an Ayurveda treatment will not consist ofsimply applying an ointment morning and evening, or drinking a magic potion, or any other action of that type. Instead, care is holistic and involves a complete revamping of our lifestyle.

It may include changesin how we feed and hydrate ourselves, as well as yoga and breathing practices, physical activity guidelines, stress management, etc. It is thus crucial that the patient fully engages in Ayurveda treatment. They will have to provide honest answers concerning his lifestyle, emotional state, level of satisfaction, quality of relationships, etc.
This care, therefore, requires that the patient take ownership of his health and have faith in the
process. For that to happen, it is essential that you, the practitioner, maintain your ability to listen with empathy and be void of judgment. You must also demonstrate an attitude of well-intentioned serenity, unaffected by your own personal considerations.

**Six Stages of Disease**

An individual who is experienced in Ayurveda will be able to recognize the six stages by simply taking
the patient’s pulse. We willsoon look at a variety of other techniques that can verify this information
in order to ensure objectivity.

But before that, we need to define the six stages. It should be noted that in western allopathic
medicine, treatment only begins in stages five or six when the disease is already established. One of
the primary advantages of Ayurvedic intervention is that it happens before that point. Thanks to this
approach, the condition is often arrested before it fully manifests itself. Here are the six stages of
disease:

**Accumulation (Sanchya)**
At this stage, a toxin may accumulate in a specific area of the body. There may be a dosha imbalance
(vital energies in Ayurveda) resulting, for example, from an emotion that we are repressing. This

accumulation prevents our energies from circulating normally, and thus a blockage appears. The sub-
doshas, (which we will discuss later) correspond to the area where the problem appears. The body must work to restore balance in that area.

**Aggravation (Prakopa)**
The blockage is complete and impermeable. Where energy circulation was difficult in the previous
stage, it is now simply not happening at all. Accumulation, therefore, becomes more critical. The
body is depleting its resources to repair the problem, yet still maintaining balance at a surface level.
An untrained eye would not observe any strong or clear symptoms.

**Spread (Prasra)**
The body’s defenses are no longer able to handle the accumulation. The accumulation can no longer
be contained in its original seat and will start to spread toxins to other parts of the body or to block
the flow of energy elsewhere. Let’s use the analogy of a road accident that prevents traffic from
flowing at an intersection. As a result, traffic will get backed up, and drivers will take alternative
routes, which may become congested as well. Similarly, toxins invade and weaken other areas of the
body.

**Localization (Sthana Samshraya)**
A previously compromised organ or tissue will be mainly targeted and weakened. The body alone is
unable to repairthe damage. We start to notice the firstsigns of disease, which are generalsymptoms
such as fatigue, paleness, apathy, etc.

**Manifestation (Vyakti)**
Typical symptoms associated with the disease start to appear. We are able to understand what we
are dealing with. The patient is able to describe sensations and precise symptoms.

**Differentiation (Bheda)**
The symptoms of the disease are fully felt. They may become chronic.

One symptom becomes more prevalent than the others, like a headache, for example. Then, if like in
this example, it is due to fatigue, we usually go to bed and ultimately re-establish balance by getting
back to our normal rhythm. In this case, we would not proceed to the following stages.

Plan of Care

As we have seen, Ayurvedic care requires us to take a lot of things into account to ensure an optimal
and constant state of good health. Therefore, to do it right and not forget anything, we will follow an
8-step treatment plan. Here it is:

1. The doshic constitution test, that is to say, the Vikruti. To find out what it is, we will give the
   person the same test you completed in Module 2, by asking him to tell us about his current state
   of health.
2. The original constitution test, that is to say, the Prakriti which will allow us to determine the
   balance towards which we must tend to help the patient attain optimal health.
3. The next step is the physical examination. This can include taking a pulse, observing the eyes and
   tongue, and an oral interview. We will seek to identify any recent changes that may have
   disrupted his balance. Those might include a change in lifestyle or diet, emotional distress, a
   change in his relationships, etc.
4. We will explore, along with the person, ways to rectify the imbalance. It is important to identify
   solutions that are appropriate and agreeable to the patient.
5. We will establish a written plan. This will include lifestyle and diet recommendations that take
   into account the patient’s deep nature (Prakriti), as well as his age, the climate, and the current
   season. These factors can also influence the doshas.
6. We will plan for detoxification of the body.
7. We will establish a program of renewal in order to strengthen organs affected by the imbalance,
   improve immunity, and thus avoid any relapse.
8. We will identify treatments appropriate to the patient’s condition.
   The first two steps of this plan are described in Module 2. You have already established your first
   doshic constitution profiles with the help of people around you. In this module, we will delve deeper
   by going to step 3 and focusing on specific body parts.

We start by observing, watching for the signs of the deterioration of the state of health. Many factors
will provide information on how well the body is performing. For the experienced practitioner, the
body is an open book. One must simply learn its language and help it find its natural state of health.

**Observing Urine and Stool**

Examining these two types of bodily waste (these two Malas) helps us determine a variety of things.
For example, if we are sick, it tells us what issues are affecting our doshas, and the severity of the
disease.

**Urine (Mutra)**
Let’s start with a brief review of what we learned in Module 3. Urine is the liquid coming from the
large intestine, where assimilation, absorption, and nutrient selection takes place. This liquid residue
goes to the liver to be filtered. The residual waste is then transported as urine to the bladder. Urine
allows waste to be eliminated outside of the body. This prevents it from accumulating internally. For
example, it removes water,salt, and other nitrogenous waste. This Mala (or bodily waste) is essential
for the maintenance of blood volume, a healthy level of electrolytes, and therefore a good blood
pressure and proper arterial tension.

Collect your morning urine in a clean, transparent container used only for this purpose. Observe the

Of course, if you are in perfect health, the color of your urine should reflect your doshic constitution
(Prakriti), as determined by the test. If not, the color will give you clues as to the nature of the

**Practical Exercise: Determining the Severity of the Disorder**
Now, put a drop of sesame oil in the same urine sample. This will determine the severity of the
imbalance and, again, for a second time, verify the nature of the problem.
If the drop dissolves and divides immediately, the disease will be easy to treat.
However, if it starts to sink and stays suspended in the middle of the urine sample, it will be
difficult to treat.
Finally, if it sinks to the bottom of the sample, the problem is serious and well established. It will
be tough to treat.
Determining the nature of the problem
If the drop creates waves on the surface of the urine, the disorder comes from the Vata dosha.
If we see reflections of various colors in these waves, we will rather suspect a Pitta imbalance.
Finally, if the drop dividesinto many little drops on the surface, the problem is a Kapha imbalance.

**Stool (Purisha)**

This Mala is a good indicator of our health. In the absence of a recognized pathology, stool can
provide valuable clues as to our diet and lifestyle. In fact, the nature of stool can be impacted by
inadequate nutrition, a trip (for example, a long, air-conditioned plane flight),too much coffee intake,
fatigue, excessive physical exercise, staying up late at night, holding on to one’s stool, emotional
factors, etc.
Stool can show the strength of our digestive fire Agni. As we have discussed, Agni must neither be
too strong nor too weak to guarantee adequate nutrition and good immunity for the body.
Moreover, the stool’s condition, its form, consistency, and odor will be affected by changes in the
dosha balance. Let’s look at this in more detail.

**Odor**
Our sense of smell will lead us to one of two conclusions. Smelly stool odors can indicate either an
aggravation of the Pitta dosha or a substantial accumulation of toxins in the body.

**Consistency**
An excess of Vata will manifest itself as broken up stool (“rabbit pellets”), dry and hard stool, or on
the other hand, completely liquid stool. However, a stool that is too soft, that looks like diarrhea -
without being totally liquid - can indicate a worsening of Pitta. This might appear after eating a spicy
meal. Finally, “rare” stools that are slow to move, fatty and sometimes accompanied by mucus can
indicate an imbalance related to Kapha.

**Color**
Ideally, stool will be brown in color. Color variations can indicate a variation in doshic balances. We
must be careful to ensure that this change in color is not caused by something else. Indeed, certain
foods, medications, or pathologies are equally capable of changing the color. If we have ruled out
those factors, we can consider a darker stool to indicate an excess of Vata. If the stool borders on
red, yellow or green, a Pitta imbalance is likely the cause. Finally, a pale stool is definitively indicative
of a problem with Kapha.
Please note that many diseases can cause lasting changes to the nature of the stool. If the anomaly
persists, consult a doctor right away. A more comprehensive diagnosis is imperative.

**Tongue Examination**

The tongue indicates how well the organs are working in our body. Thus, by carefully observing its
size, shape, appearance, and color, we can collect valuable clues about the state of one’s health.

The sides of the tongue, which we are unable to see on the diagram, relate to digestion. This digestion
can be physical (digesting food), as well as psychic (digesting or understanding events).
In essence, any particularity or anomaly that appears on an area of our tongue informs us about a
change or disorder affecting one of our organs.*

![affections](../Ayurveda%20Practicioner/images/module05-tongue-affections.png)

Observing the color of the tongue can also give us valuable information. If the tongue is pale, we can
suspect anemia. If it is blue, we should focus on the heart. If it is yellow, we will look at the liver.
Moreover, certain colors can indicate a doshic imbalance.
For example, a whitish tongue with an accumulation of mucus tells us that there is a problem with
Kapha. If it is more reddish, yellowish, or greenish, we can suspect an imbalance of Pitta. Finally, if it
is dark, brown or black, the problem lies with the Vata dosha.


**Facial Examination**

The most expressive part of our body is our face. It reveals our emotions, and these emotions have
an impact on our appearance. That is why we also refer to this body part as the "mirror of the soul."
Our facial lines reveal much about our state of mind.
For example, horizontal lines across our forehead result from repeated anxiety and worries. But that
is not all. Our face is also linked to our organs and is able to reflect on how they are working.

![facial]()

Thus, the vertical line buried between our two brows is the result of repressed emotions that
negatively affect the functioning of our liver. Swelling under the eyes indicates a renal disorder.
By observing the areas of the face where there are pimples, discoloration, itching orswelling, we can
tell which organs to focus on in order to stay in optimal health.

Lip Examination

Lips are an area of our body that has multiple roles (e.g., the seat of sensations, communication
organ, etc.). They are highly sensitive and can reveal many secrets about what is happening inside
our bodies. It isimportant to carefully observe theirsize,shape, color, and the appearance of the skin
surrounding them.

Dry and rugged lips are characteristic of Vata, but they can also indicate an imbalance in that dosha,
nervousness, or momentary fear. Repeated herpes outbreaks or inflammatory pimples around the
contour of the lips indicate a Pitta imbalance.
Pale lips alert us to anemia. Our lips are also divided into areas that are each related to one of our
organs. Once again, these anomalies lead us to further investigate what is wrong.
